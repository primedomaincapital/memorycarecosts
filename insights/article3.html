Medicaid and Memory Care: What Gets Covered

Here's the statistic that reframes every conversation about memory care costs: Medicaid pays for roughly 60% of all long-term care in America. Not private savings. Not insurance. Medicaid — the government program most middle-class families assume is "for poor people" — is the primary funder of dementia care in the United States.

5 minute read · October 2026

Understanding how this works isn't optional for families facing memory care costs. It's the difference between a planned transition and a financial catastrophe.

The Basic Framework

Medicaid is a joint federal-state program, which means the rules vary by state — sometimes dramatically. But the core structure is consistent:

Medicaid covers long-term custodial care — including memory care in licensed facilities — for eligible individuals. This is the critical distinction from Medicare, which does not cover custodial care. If your parent needs memory care and qualifies for Medicaid, the program pays the facility directly.

Eligibility has two tests:

  1. Medical/functional: The applicant must need a nursing-home level of care, as determined by a state assessment. For dementia patients requiring memory care, this test is almost always met — the diagnosis and functional impairment qualify.
  1. Financial: The applicant must have limited income and assets. The asset limit is typically $2,000 in countable assets. Income limits vary by state but generally must be below the cost of care (if income exceeds the limit, Miller Trusts / Qualified Income Trusts can bridge the gap in most states).

Countable vs. Exempt Assets

Not everything counts toward the $2,000 limit:

Exempt (don't count):

  • Primary residence (up to $713,000 in equity in most states for 2026, if the applicant or spouse lives there or intends to return)
  • One vehicle
  • Personal belongings and household goods
  • Prepaid funeral/burial arrangements (up to state limits, typically $10,000–$15,000)
  • Certain life insurance (small face-value policies)

Countable (must be spent down):

  • Bank accounts, investments, retirement accounts
  • Second properties
  • Cash value of large life insurance policies

The spend-down process — legally reducing countable assets to $2,000 — is where elder-law attorneys earn their fees. Done correctly, it's orderly and planned. Done in crisis, it's chaotic and often involves penalties.

The Five-Year Lookback: The Rule That Punishes Procrastination

Medicaid examines all asset transfers in the five years before application. Any gifts or below-market transfers during this period trigger a penalty — a period of ineligibility calculated by dividing the transferred amount by the state's average monthly care cost.

Example: A parent gifted $100,000 to children three years before applying for Medicaid in a state where the penalty divisor is $10,000/month. Penalty: 10 months of ineligibility. During those 10 months, Medicaid won't pay — and the $100,000 is already gone.

This is why elder-law attorneys say the best time to plan was five years ago, and the second-best time is now. Every month of delay extends the penalty window.

Exceptions that don't trigger penalties:

  • Transfers to a spouse (unlimited)
  • Transfers to a disabled child
  • Transfers to a caregiver child who lived in the home for 2+ years and delayed institutionalization
  • Transfers to certain special needs trusts

Spousal Protections

When one spouse needs memory care and the other remains in the community, federal law provides protections against total impoverishment:

Spousal asset allowance: The community spouse can retain between $30,828 and $154,140 in assets (2026 federal ranges; states set specific figures within this band).

Monthly maintenance needs allowance: The community spouse can retain sufficient income to meet basic living expenses — minimum $2,555/month, up to $3,854/month depending on state and documented need.

These protections are essential but widely misunderstood. They don't make Medicaid planning unnecessary — they make it survivable. An elder-law attorney ensures the healthy spouse retains the maximum allowed, not the minimum.

What Medicaid Actually Covers in Memory Care

Once eligible, Medicaid covers:

  • Room and board in a Medicaid-certified memory care facility
  • All custodial care (assistance with ADLs, supervision, behavioral management)
  • Medication management
  • Basic medical supplies

Important caveats:

  • Not all facilities accept Medicaid. Many premium memory care communities are private-pay only. The Medicaid-certified options may have waitlists. Planning the transition early — identifying Medicaid-accepting facilities before they're needed — prevents crisis placement.
  • The resident contributes income. Medicaid doesn't let the resident keep their Social Security check — most income goes to the facility as the "patient pay amount," with the resident retaining a small personal needs allowance ($30–$200/month depending on state).
  • Estate recovery. After death, states must seek reimbursement from the deceased's estate for Medicaid long-term care expenditures. The home — often the largest remaining asset — is the primary target. Proper planning (transferring the home to exempt beneficiaries, Lady Bird deeds in applicable states) addresses this.

The Planning Timeline

"Medicaid planning isn't about hiding money. It's about using the rules Congress wrote to protect families from the catastrophic cost of long-term care. The rules are complex because the problem is complex — and navigating them without guidance is the most expensive mistake families make."

5+ years before need: Ideal planning window. Asset protection trusts, strategic gifting, and restructuring can be completed without lookback penalties.

2–5 years before need: Still workable. Some strategies available; the lookback creates constraints but doesn't eliminate options.

Crisis planning (need is immediate): Options exist — promissory notes, annuities, spousal transfers — but they're narrower, more expensive (attorney fees $5,000–$15,000), and more stressful. This is where most families find themselves, and it's the worst place to be.

The Bottom Line

Medicaid is the backbone of America's long-term care financing, whether families acknowledge it or not. The program's complexity isn't a bug — it reflects the genuine difficulty of means-testing catastrophic care costs. But complexity rewards preparation and punishes delay. Every family facing potential memory care needs should consult an elder-law attorney before the crisis, not during it. The five-year clock is always ticking.
