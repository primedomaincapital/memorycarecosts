Veterans Benefits for Memory Care: What the VA Actually Pays

Prime Domain Capital Research · 7 minute read · November 2026

The daughter did the arithmetic the way families do it: on the back of the brochure, in the parking lot, before she had finished crying. Her father was eighty-seven, a Navy veteran of Korea, two years into Alzheimer's, and the memory care community near her house wanted $7,900 a month. He had a pension, Social Security, and about $140,000 left. Somewhere she had read that the VA helps veterans pay for this. She went home and searched, and what she found was a benefit with a name she had never heard — Aid and Attendance — a monthly amount that looked substantial, and a set of rules that made the amount smaller the moment she understood them. The VA does pay for memory care. It does not pay for most of it. The difference between those two sentences is the whole story.

Aid and Attendance is not a care program. It is a pension enhancement — extra money added to a wartime veteran's VA pension, tax-free, every month, to help pay for long-term care. The veteran has to have served during a wartime period, has to need help with daily activities or be bedridden or in a nursing home, and has to meet a net worth limit. The money can be spent on memory care, assisted living, or in-home care. It cannot be spent on anything else the VA decides is not care. And the amount, while real, is a fraction of the bill it is meant to offset.

The amounts, and the gap

For the period December 1, 2025 through November 30, 2026, the maximum monthly Aid and Attendance amounts are $2,424 for a single veteran, $2,874 for a veteran with one dependent, $1,558 for a surviving spouse with no dependents, and $3,845 for two married veterans who both qualify. Those are maximums — the ceiling, not the check. The actual payment is the VA's Maximum Annual Pension Rate minus the veteran's countable annual income, divided by twelve. A veteran with $1,500 a month in countable income does not receive $2,424. He receives the difference between the maximum and what he already has, and the care costs he is paying are what reduce his countable income in the first place. The arithmetic rewards the families who document every dollar of care spending, and punishes the ones who do not.

Set those numbers against the bill. The national median for memory care in 2026 runs $7,440 to $8,060 a month, modeled from the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey's assisted living median of $6,200 with the standard 20 to 30 percent memory care premium. In Mississippi the range is $5,243 to $5,680. In Hawaii it is $14,515 to $15,725. A single veteran receiving the maximum $2,424 covers about a third of the national median bill — and less than a fifth in Hawaii. A surviving spouse receiving $1,558 covers less than a quarter. The benefit is the largest single source of help most families will find outside Medicaid, and it still leaves a gap of roughly $5,000 a month that has to come from savings, family contributions, or a spend-down to Medicaid. That gap is the reason the average memory care stay — two to three years — turns a monthly rate into a lifetime bill of $186,000 to $290,000, and why 7.2 million Americans aged 65 and older now live with Alzheimer's disease while families carry about 70 percent of the $405,262 lifetime cost of care for one person with dementia.

Who qualifies, and what stops them

Eligibility has three gates, and families trip on all three. The first is service: the veteran must have served at least 90 days of active duty, with at least one day during a wartime period, and must have been discharged under conditions other than dishonorable. Korea, Vietnam, the Gulf War, and the post-9/11 conflicts all count; the VA publishes the qualifying dates, and they are broader than most families assume. A veteran who served in peacetime only does not qualify, no matter how severe the need. The second gate is medical: the veteran must need help with daily activities — bathing, dressing, eating, toileting — or be bedridden, or be a patient in a nursing home, or be nearly blind. Dementia alone does not automatically qualify; the VA needs a physician's statement, on VA Form 21-2680, documenting the specific help required. A diagnosis without a functional assessment is an incomplete application.

The third gate is financial, and it is the one that surprises families most. The net worth limit for the period ending November 30, 2026 is $163,699, counting assets plus annual income, and excluding the primary home, one vehicle, and basic household furnishings. A veteran with $140,000 in savings and $30,000 a year in income is over the limit. A veteran with the same savings and $12,000 a year in income may not be. The limit is adjusted every December 1 — the 2026 rates rose 2.8 percent on the prior year, and the VA has announced a 3.6 percent increase effective December 1, 2026 — so a family that is over the limit in November may be under it in December, and a family that qualifies this year may not qualify next year if the limit does not keep pace with their income. The net worth test is also the reason families are told, correctly, not to give assets away to qualify: the VA applies a 36-month look-back on asset transfers, and a transfer made to get under the limit can disqualify the veteran for years.

The application, and the wait

The application itself is VA Form 21P-527EZ for veterans and 21P-534EZ for surviving spouses, filed with the 21-2680 examination form, proof of service, proof of medical need, and a full accounting of income, assets, and care costs. The VA does not publish a processing time for Aid and Attendance, and the honest range families report is three to six months from filing to first payment, with some claims taking a year. The payment, when it arrives, is retroactive to the application date — but the memory care community does not wait for the VA, and the months between filing and payment are paid out of pocket.

The documentation mistakes that delay approval are specific and avoidable. The most common is the physician's statement: a form that says "Alzheimer's disease" without describing what the veteran cannot do — cannot bathe alone, cannot dress alone, cannot manage medication — is not enough, and the VA will ask for more. The second is incomplete care-cost documentation: the VA needs the facility's statement of charges, not the family's estimate, and it needs every month accounted for. The third is income: families forget to report a small pension, or a rental payment, or a spouse's income, and the discrepancy triggers a development letter that adds weeks. The fourth is the simplest: the application is filed without the discharge papers. The VA can request them, but the request takes time, and the claim sits while it waits.

There is a counterargument to the whole enterprise, and families should hear it before they spend months on paperwork. Aid and Attendance is a pension for wartime veterans, not a long-term care program, and its amounts were never set by reference to what memory care costs. The $2,424 maximum for a single veteran was set by the pension rate, not by the market. A family that spends six months securing $2,424 a month against a $7,900 bill has still solved less than a third of the problem, and the time spent on the application is time not spent on the harder decisions — whether to sell the house, whether to apply for Medicaid, whether the community is the right one at all. The benefit is worth claiming. It is not worth treating as the plan.

That said, the math changes when the benefit is combined with the other pieces families miss. A veteran who qualifies for Aid and Attendance may also qualify for VA health care, which covers some medical costs the pension does not. A surviving spouse who does not qualify for Aid and Attendance may qualify for the VA's housebound benefit, a smaller enhancement. And the House passed a bill in May 2026, H.R. 6047, that would add $833 a month on top of Aid and Attendance for qualifying veterans — it has not become law, and families should not budget for it, but it is a sign that Congress knows the gap exists. The benefit is one instrument in a set, and the families who do best are the ones who treat it that way: file the claim, document everything, and plan as if the check will be smaller and later than the maximum suggests. It usually is. It is still worth having.
