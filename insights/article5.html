7.2 Million Reasons the Memory Care Bill Keeps Growing

5 minute read · November 2026

The arithmetic usually happens at a kitchen table, late, after the tour. A daughter has just walked through a memory care community outside Columbus or Charlotte or Phoenix, and the brochure number is still in her head. Then she does the real math: the base rate, the care-level add-on, the medication management fee, the incontinence care charge, and the months — how many months — her mother’s savings will last at that burn rate. The answer is almost always smaller than she expected. The reason is not that any single price is unreasonable. It is that the population needing the care is growing faster than the money set aside to pay for it.

The Alzheimer’s Association’s 2025 Facts and Figures report, released April 29, 2025, put the scale in plain terms: for the first time, the number of Americans living with Alzheimer’s disease has passed 7 million. The estimate is 7.2 million people aged 65 and older — about 1 in 9 in that age group — and the total is projected to reach 13.8 million by 2060 without a medical breakthrough. Every one of those people will eventually need the kind of supervision that memory care sells.

The price, as the surveys measure it

The CareScout Cost of Care Survey for 2025 — the benchmark formerly published under the Genworth name, based on more than 15,000 completed surveys across all 50 states with data collected between July and November 2025, and released March 2, 2026 — puts the national median for assisted living at $6,200 a month, or $74,400 a year. Memory care is not surveyed as its own line. The industry’s working rule, from the National Center for Assisted Living, is that memory care runs 20% to 30% above standard assisted living, reflecting higher staff ratios, secured environments and specialized dementia training. At the 2025 median, that puts memory care at roughly $7,130 to $8,060 a month — and a 25% premium, the midpoint most estimators use, lands at about $7,750.

Other surveys land in the same range from different directions. A Place for Mom’s 2026 data puts memory care at $6,690 a month against $5,419 for assisted living inside the same survey — a 23% premium, consistent with the industry rule. The alternatives are not cheap either: full-time home care runs $6,673 a month at the 2025 national median, a semi-private nursing home room $9,581, a private room $10,798. There is no inexpensive setting for round-the-clock dementia supervision. There are only settings that bill differently.

The demand wave underneath

The cost figures describe a market. The prevalence figures describe a wave. Total annual costs of caring for people living with Alzheimer’s and other dementias are projected at $384 billion in 2025, excluding unpaid care — and Medicare and Medicaid pay $246 billion of that, or 64%, while out-of-pocket spending runs $97 billion, a quarter of the total. Deaths due to Alzheimer’s disease more than doubled between 2000 and 2022. The baby boom generation, which spent its working life accumulating the housing wealth that pays for care, is now entering the age band where dementia prevalence climbs steeply.

The workforce math is just as tight. Nearly 12 million family members and friends provide 19.2 billion hours of unpaid care, valued at an additional $413 billion. That is the subsidy the formal market never sees on an invoice — and it is the first thing to crack when a caregiver’s own health fails or a job cannot bend any further. When it cracks, the family does not shop for memory care. It buys it, on a hospital discharge deadline, in 72 hours.

Who actually pays

The lifetime cost of care for one person with dementia is estimated at $405,262, and about 70% of it is borne by families in the forms of unpaid caregiving and out-of-pocket spending. Medicaid covers memory care in some states and some circumstances, but it is a patchwork that rewards spending down assets first — which is why the families who plan earliest, while the parent can still participate in the decisions, end up with the most options and the least regret. Long-term care insurance, bought a decade before it is needed, changes the arithmetic completely. Bought a year before, it is usually unavailable at any price.

The case for waiting — and for staying home

There is a genuine counterargument to the rush into memory care, and it is getting stronger. Nearly 4 in 5 Americans say they would want to know if they had Alzheimer’s before it interfered with their lives, and the 2025 report documents new treatments that address the underlying biology and slow memory decline for people diagnosed in the early stages. Early detection changes the timeline: a person diagnosed early can plan, can choose, and can stay home longer with support. Full-time home care at $6,673 a month is not cheap, but for a couple still living together it can be cheaper than two moves and a facility contract — and it keeps the person in the place they know.

The honest version of that argument has a limit. Home care works until the supervision need becomes round-the-clock, and dementia is a disease that moves in one direction. The families who wait longest are often the ones who pay most: a crisis placement at the highest care level, with no time to compare communities, is the most expensive way to buy memory care. The decision is not whether to pay. It is when, and on whose terms.

Plan while the bill is still hypothetical. Price the community, the care-level ladder, and the home-care alternative before the diagnosis forces the choice. The 7.2 million number will be larger next year, the $405,262 figure will be larger too, and the families who did the arithmetic at the kitchen table — early, together, with real numbers — will be the ones who can still afford to choose.
