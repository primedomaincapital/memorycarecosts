How Long Do Families Typically Pay for Memory Care?

Prime Domain Capital Research · 9 minute read · November 2026

The notepad came out at the kitchen table, the way it always does. A son in Ohio had just toured a memory care community for his mother, eighty-two, diagnosed two years earlier, and the number on the brochure was $7,900 a month. He wrote it down. Underneath it he wrote $300,000 — roughly what his mother had left, house excluded — and did the division. Thirty-eight months. Three years and two months. Then he put the pen down, because the division was the easy part. The hard part was the question underneath it: thirty-eight months of what? The brochure priced a month. Nobody would price the stay.

That is the question this briefing answers, and the honest version of the answer is a range, not a number. The industry’s working consensus, repeated across operator guides and placement agencies, is that the average memory care stay runs two to three years. A Place for Mom, the largest senior living marketplace in the country, widens that to two to ten years — a range so broad it looks like a refusal to answer, until you understand what it is measuring. The stay is not one thing. It is the overlap of two clocks that run at different speeds: the disease, and the body carrying it.

Two clocks, one stay

The disease clock is the slower and more predictable of the two. The Alzheimer’s Association puts average survival at four to eight years after an Alzheimer’s diagnosis, with some people living as long as twenty. A 2021 meta-analysis of 63,125 people with dementia, published in the peer-reviewed literature, put the figure at 5.8 years. Measured from the first symptoms rather than the diagnosis — which often comes late — the average stretches to eight to ten years. Age at diagnosis moves the number more than anything else: people diagnosed between 65 and 80 tend to survive seven to ten years, while those diagnosed after 80 average roughly three to four.

The stages inside that span have their own rough durations. On the Global Deterioration Scale developed by Dr. Barry Reisberg and colleagues, mild dementia lasts about two years, moderately severe dementia about a year and a half, severe dementia about two and a half, and the final, very severe stage six years or more for those who reach it. The middle stage is typically the longest, the Alzheimer’s Association notes, and it is the stage that fills most memory care beds: the person needs help with dressing, bathing and toileting, wanders, wakes at night, and can no longer be left alone — but may live in that condition for years.

The second clock is the body, and it is what cuts most stays short of the disease’s full span. People do not move into memory care at diagnosis. They move in when home stops being safe — often years into the disease, often after a crisis — so the stay measures the tail of the illness, not its whole length. And the stay ends for reasons that have little to do with dementia’s schedule: a fall, pneumonia, a heart condition that was always going to set the limit. That is why the averages disagree with each other. The ALIS 500, a benchmarking dataset drawn from more than 30,000 resident records across 500 senior living communities, covering assisted living, memory care and independent living, reported in the third quarter of 2025 an average length of stay of 493 days against a median of 258 days, with 58 percent of residents moving out within a year. The mean and the median are telling different stories: most stays are short, and a minority of very long stays pulls the average up. Both numbers are true. Neither, on its own, is a plan. And the ALIS figure is not a memory-care-only number — it should not be quoted as one — but the shape it describes, a short median and a long tail, is the shape every memory care operator recognizes.

Turning months into money

Put a price on the range and the planning problem comes into focus. Take $7,750 a month — the midpoint of the memory care range implied by the CareScout 2025 national median for assisted living, $6,200, marked up by the industry’s standard 20 to 30 percent premium. Two years at that rate is $186,000. Three years is $279,000. Five years is $465,000. Eight years — the top of the Alzheimer’s Association’s average survival range — is $744,000. Ten years, the tail a family with an early-onset diagnosis must genuinely consider, is $930,000. The National Investment Center for Seniors Housing & Care put the national average at $7,899 a month in 2023 — the same order of magnitude, from a different year and a different method. These are flat-rate figures, and flat rates flatter the bill: NIC reported memory care asking rents in rental retirement communities rising 4.3 percent in the year to the third quarter of 2025, to $8,238 a month. At that growth rate, a five-year stay that starts at $7,750 a month costs about $507,000 in total, not $465,000 — and a three-year stay costs about $291,000, not $279,000.

Set those totals against the lifetime figure the Alzheimer’s Association published in its 2025 report: $405,262 for one person with dementia, with about 70 percent of it borne by families through unpaid caregiving and out-of-pocket spending. The $405,262 is a lifetime average across every setting, including years at home; the memory care bill is concentrated into the years when the person needs the most expensive setting. Total spending on Alzheimer’s and other dementias is projected at $384 billion in 2025, with $97 billion of it out of pocket. Medicare does not pay for the room, the board or the supervision — it pays for medical care — so the monthly rate lands, in most families, on savings, on home equity, or on children.

The tail is the plan

The tail is where the planning either holds or collapses. A family that budgets for the average stay — two to three years, call it $186,000 to $279,000 — is budgeting for the outcome that is most likely and least dangerous. The dangerous outcome is the long one: the parent diagnosed at 68 who lives to 84, the spouse who stays healthy enough to need care for a decade, the person whose body outlasts their mind by years. Long-term care insurance, bought a decade before it is needed, exists precisely for this shape of risk; bought a year before, it is usually unavailable at any price. Medicaid exists for the other end of the tail, but it is a spend-down program — it rewards arriving with little left, which is why the families who plan earliest keep the most options.

Demand is moving against the family that waits. Memory care led absorption among continuing care retirement communities in the second quarter of 2026, according to NIC, and rental communities’ memory care rents rose 4.3 percent in a year. A bed that costs $7,750 a month today costs more next year, and the communities with the strongest staffing are the ones with the waiting lists. There is also a newer pressure on the timeline, and it cuts against the family’s interests: the anti-amyloid drugs — Eisai and Biogen’s lecanemab and Eli Lilly’s donanemab — slow cognitive decline in early-stage patients. That is unambiguously good news for patients. It also stretches the mild stage, which stretches the years a family pays for care. Medicine that slows the disease does not shrink the bill. It reschedules it.

The case for the short plan

There is a real argument for planning short, and it deserves more than a dismissal. Most stays are short — the ALIS median of 258 days says so, and the families in the Alzheimer’s Association’s own caregiver forums describe stays of two months, three months, a single season. Death, senior living executives told Senior Housing News, is the leading cause of move-outs in memory care. A family that locks away $500,000 for a ten-year stay that lasts eleven months has not been prudent; it has been afraid, and the money it immobilized could have paid for better home care, a safer house, or the surviving spouse’s own retirement. And there is a subtler point: moving in early, while the person can still settle and form attachments, tends to mean a longer stay. Families that wait for the crisis get the short stay they planned for — at the highest care level, at the highest price, with no time to choose.

The honest answer is that both plans are wrong on their own. The short plan fails in the tail. The long plan fails the present. What works is a plan in three parts: fund the likely stay — two to three years at the local rate, priced at the top of the local range — from money that is actually there; decide in advance what happens if the stay runs to five years, which is the point where most families have to sell a house or qualify for Medicaid; and insure the tail, through long-term care insurance bought early, a Medicaid plan made with an elder law attorney, or a family agreement about who provides care and who pays. Write down the triggers — the month the savings hit a number, the care level that forces a move — while everyone can still agree on them.

The son in Ohio did one more piece of arithmetic before he put the notepad away. Thirty-eight months at $7,900 was $300,200 — almost exactly what his mother had. The number was not a coincidence; it was the shape of the problem. The monthly rate is the price of a month. The stay is the price of the disease. Families that plan for the month run out of money in the third year and call it bad luck. Families that plan for the stay — the likely years, the long tail, and the line between them — run out of surprises instead. That is the only part of this bill anyone gets to control.
