Memory Care Costs by State: Where the Same Care Costs Twice as Much

Prime Domain Capital Research · 8 minute read · November 2026

The call came on a Tuesday night, the kind of call that starts with a number. A woman in Burlington, Vermont, had just toured a memory care community for her mother: $11,400 a month, before the care-level add-ons. Her sister, three states south in Birmingham, Alabama, had toured a comparable community the week before: $5,600. Same diagnosis. Same locked doors, same trained staff, same private room with a window onto a garden. The difference between the two quotes was $5,800 a month — $69,600 a year — for what was, on paper, the same care. Neither sister had done anything wrong. Neither community was gouging. They had simply discovered, the expensive way, that in memory care the price is not set by the care. It is set by the map.

The national benchmarks make the gap look abstract until you put two states side by side. The CareScout Cost of Care Survey for 2025 — the benchmark formerly published under the Genworth name, fielded from July through November 2025 across more than 4,900 assisted living communities and released March 2, 2026 — puts the national median for a private one-bedroom assisted living unit at $6,200 a month, or $74,400 a year. The state medians underneath it run from $4,369 a month in Mississippi to $12,096 in Hawaii. That is a 2.8-times spread on the same product, and an annual difference of $92,724 between the cheapest and the most expensive state in the country.

Memory care multiplies the map

CareScout does not publish a separate memory care line, so the memory care figures have to be built, not quoted. The National Center for Assisted Living’s working rule — the one every estimator in the industry uses — is that memory care runs 20 to 30 percent above standard assisted living, reflecting higher staff ratios, secured environments and specialized dementia training. Apply that rule to the 2025 state medians and the map stretches further: at the national median, memory care lands at roughly $7,440 to $8,060 a month. In Mississippi it lands at $5,243 to $5,680. In Hawaii it lands at $14,515 to $15,725. The spread is the same ratio, but the dollars are not: the 25 percent premium that costs a Mississippi family about $1,092 a month costs a Hawaii family about $3,024.

Two surveys that measure memory care directly tell the same story at different levels — which is itself useful information. A Place for Mom’s 2026 report, built on actual move-in costs in its partner communities during 2025, puts the national median for memory care at $6,690 a month, against $5,419 for assisted living inside the same dataset: a 23 percent premium, squarely inside the industry rule. Its state medians run from $4,806 a month in Utah and $5,000 in Georgia to $11,195 in Vermont, $9,840 in Massachusetts and $9,665 in New Jersey. SeniorLiving.org, which combined community pricing with survey responses from families paying for care in 2026, puts the national median higher, at $8,019 a month, with Hawaii at $14,399 and South Dakota at $5,538 — a 2.6-times spread. The three sources disagree about the level. They agree about the geography: the Northeast and Hawaii at the top, the Southeast and parts of the Mountain West at the bottom. Seven of SeniorLiving.org’s ten most expensive states are in the Northeast; five of its ten cheapest are in the Southeast. The scale behind the demand is national: the USC Schaeffer Institute estimates Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias will cost the United States $818 billion in 2026.

Why the premium itself varies

The part of the story most guides miss is that the memory care premium is not a fixed markup. It varies by state, and that variation is what makes the memory care map wider than the assisted living map. In A Place for Mom’s 2026 data, memory care costs 19 percent more than assisted living in California ($6,850 against $5,739) and 19 percent more in Florida ($5,495 against $4,624), but 42 percent more in Vermont ($11,195 against $7,885), 36 percent more in Massachusetts and Mississippi, and 48 percent more in Minnesota ($7,880 against $5,310). The premium is doing independent work — and it is biggest where the care is hardest to staff.

Labor is the first driver, and it is visible in the hourly numbers. A Place for Mom’s state data puts home care at $25 an hour in Mississippi and $43 an hour in Vermont — the same hour of the same work, priced 72 percent higher, because the labor market around it is 72 percent more expensive. Memory care is more labor than building: higher staff ratios, awake overnight staff, dementia-specific training. When wages rise, memory care rates rise faster than assisted living rates, because there is more labor in every dollar of the price.

Regulation is the second driver. States write their own staffing minimums, training requirements and physical-plant rules for secured dementia units, and the strict states cost more to operate in. The third is scarcity. Demand for memory care is outrunning supply in the places where it is already expensive: the National Investment Center for Seniors Housing & Care reported that memory care led absorption among continuing care retirement communities in the second quarter of 2026, at 1.3 percent year over year, and that rental communities were asking $8,238 a month for memory care in the third quarter of 2025, up 4.3 percent on the year. A scarce, regulated, labor-heavy product in a high-wage state does not converge toward the national median. It pulls away from it.

The case against moving for the price

None of this means a family should move a parent to the cheapest state on the map. The arithmetic in favor of moving is real — a family paying Vermont prices who moved a parent to Georgia would save roughly $6,000 a month on A Place for Mom’s medians, $72,000 a year — but the costs of the move are real too, and most of them never appear in a rate card. Medicaid, which pays for roughly 60 percent of all long-term care in America, is a state program: eligibility rules, waiver programs and waitlists do not transfer across state lines, and a family that moves a parent to a cheaper state can find itself re-qualifying from scratch. A person with dementia pays a price for the move itself — new rooms, new faces, new routines are disorienting in a way that shows up as agitation, wandering and weight loss — and the family that moved a parent away from the daughter who visits on Tuesdays has traded a lower invoice for a lonelier parent.

There is also the matter of what the state median hides. Within California, A Place for Mom’s data puts memory care at $9,500 to $11,500 a month in the San Francisco Bay Area against $6,500 to $8,500 in the Central Valley — a within-state gap nearly as wide as the national one. A family in Fresno does not pay San Francisco prices, and a family in Burlington does not pay the Vermont median unless it buys the median community. The state is a starting point for the search, not the price of any particular room.

What to do with the map

Used properly, the map changes three decisions. First, it sets the budget honestly: a family planning in Massachusetts or Hawaii should budget roughly double what a family in Mississippi or Alabama budgets for the same years of care, and a national average is the wrong number for both of them. Second, it prices the option of moving before the crisis: if a parent is willing to relocate while they can still participate in the choice, the savings are large enough to fund years of additional care — but only if the Medicaid rules, the housing sale and the family’s visiting capacity are priced into the same decision. Third, it disciplines the quote: a community charging 40 percent above its own state median should be able to say why, in staffing, in training, in outcomes — and a community charging the median in an expensive state deserves a second look at what it leaves out.

The two sisters on the phone that Tuesday night did not resolve anything. The Vermont sister could not move her mother to Birmingham, and the Birmingham sister could not move hers to Burlington. But they stopped arguing about which community was overcharging, and started arguing about the right thing: how many months each of them could pay, at the price where the parent actually lives, before the money ran out. That is the question the map answers. The price of memory care is not one number. It is fifty of them, and the family’s job is to find the one that applies — then plan as if it were the only one that mattered, because for that family, it is.
